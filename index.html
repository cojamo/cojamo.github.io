How I got started

My fascination with computers started in 1995, when my dad bought our first PC from Montgomery Ward. It was the coolest thing I had ever seen. He was understandably nervous when the first thing I wanted to do was slide the case cover off and see what was inside.

I quickly became the family IT guy, although I suspect a fair number of the "problems" I fixed were ones I had caused. Luckily, I don't think they ever figured that out.

Today, I still have that same curiosity about PC hardware. That's what drew me to homelabbing: it's the perfect mix of hardware and software. I love building a PC and getting it running, but a home network gives me something I can keep working on. There's always another service to try, a connection to troubleshoot, or something new to learn.

Inside the lab

Three HP EliteDesk machines handle dedicated roles in my lab, supported by separate systems for AI and storage.

Top machine / AI control

Hermes-Control

An HP EliteDesk 800 G3 runs my AI control setup. It connects to Ubuntu-AI, a separate machine running a local Qwen model. I also use OpenAI for harder tasks when I'm comfortable sending the information to a cloud service. This lets me experiment with local and cloud AI while choosing which tasks stay on my own hardware.

Middle machine / Networking

OPNsense

The middle EliteDesk runs OPNsense as my router and firewall. It forms the foundation of my home network, connecting the lab to the internet and managing traffic between networks.

Bottom machine / Virtualization

Ubuntu Server & Steam

The bottom EliteDesk runs Ubuntu Server and hosts a Windows virtual machine that keeps my Steam library updated. Using Steam's built-in local network transfers, it serves game files to the other gaming PCs in my home instead of each machine downloading them separately from the internet.

From spare gaming hardware to a home server

My Unraid journey started the way I suspect a lot of homelabs do: I upgraded my gaming PC and had hardware left over. In my case, that was an i9-9900K, 32 GB of RAM, and an RTX 3080.

I had been watching Hardware Haven on YouTube and saw Unraid being used for exactly the kinds of projects I wanted to try. Other people chose TrueNAS, but Unraid looked like a good fit for my first server, so I gave it a shot.

It started small with Minecraft and Rust servers. Today, it runs around 15 containers and a couple of virtual machines. It also has two GPUs with separate jobs: one runs a small language model for lighter AI tasks, while the other handles machine learning for Immich.

Immich is probably my favorite service. Having my photos stored locally gives me more peace of mind, and it makes the server useful for something beyond my latest experiment.

Running Unraid has taught me the importance of organizing files, understanding permissions, and thinking carefully about how I protect my data. Most importantly, it taught me a lesson worth remembering: RAID is not a backup.

Projects & lessons learned

Building a network I could experiment with

I started looking into OPNsense when I couldn't create the VLAN setup I wanted with my TP-Link Deco mesh router. I wanted more control over my lab network without disrupting the internet connection my family depended on.

My solution was to put an OPNsense router behind the Deco. That gave me a separate space to practice network segmentation and try new configurations without having my family yell at me every time I experimented.

I installed and configured OPNsense myself. It was much more straightforward than I expected, and my first thought afterward was that I should have done it sooner. It gave me room to move beyond the limitations of my existing setup and learn by doing.

A week troubleshooting the wrong problem

One of my most frustrating homelab experiences started when I added an Aruba access point. I got it connected and configured, but every device that joined would disconnect within a minute or two.

I spent a week working through everything I could think of. I watched YouTube videos, revisited the configuration, and used my local AI to help troubleshoot. I was fairly new to this, but I had decided the problem wasn't going to beat me.

Eventually, I replaced the access point, and the replacement worked flawlessly. Based on the symptoms and my research, I suspected a faulty radio in the original AP. I couldn't prove which component had failed, but replacing the unit resolved the issue.

That experience taught me to include hardware failure in my troubleshooting instead of assuming I had configured something incorrectly.

I still have nightmares about that AP.
